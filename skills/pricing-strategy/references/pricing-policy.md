# Pricing policy

Covers the rules for "how price moves in individual deals": discounts, exceptions, price increases, promotions, and price objections. This is the fourth stage of the value cascade (value creation → value communication → price structure → pricing policy → price level → price competition), the stage that keeps the price list from eroding at the point of sale. Use it when writing discount rules or diagnosing existing discounting practice.

## Key points

- A pricing policy is a set of rules that define the criteria for varying price by customer, period, or deal, applied consistently. Its purpose is to steer customer expectations and purchase behavior in the seller's favor.
- Once customers learn that "holding out gets a lower price," list price stops holding. The seller's own ad hoc discounting creates that expectation.
- A discount approval hierarchy is a personnel policy, not a pricing policy. Customers simply learn that "escalating gets a lower price."
- Treat an exception request as a chance to write a rule that applies to every customer with the same conditions. The question is: "Would we still profit if we offered this price to every customer with the same conditions?"
- Every concession must be a give–get (conceding something in exchange for something). Do not lower price without getting volume, contract length, reduced service, prepayment, a published case study, or the like.
- Grant concessions through timing, incremental volume, lump sums, or time-limited credits, and keep the base price intact. A concession price becomes the starting point for the next negotiation.
- Manage by pocket price (net price after off-invoice concessions), not invoice price. On the price waterfall, which lines up the erosion from list price step by step, the less visible a concession is, the more it grows.

## Decision criteria and when to use what

### Ad hoc pricing vs. policy-based pricing

| Aspect | Ad hoc (reactive) | Policy-based (proactive) |
|---|---|---|
| Basis for discounts | Customer resistance, approver discretion | Conditions set in advance |
| What customers learn | Resistance, threats, and escalation pay off | Price moves only in trade-offs against value |
| Outcome | A vicious cycle that erodes price integrity (the trust that other customers pay the quoted price too) | A virtuous cycle where customers disclose information and new options emerge |

### Handling objections by buyer type

The axes are the importance of differentiation and search cost (how hard it is to compare) ([1] Exhibit 5-4).

| Type | Position | Policy |
|---|---|---|
| Value buyer | Differentiation important, search easy | Unbundle the sources of value and trade through give–get. Give sales options other than "back down" or "concede for free" |
| Brand buyer | Differentiation important, search hard | Do not discount. Answer disappointment with compensation (such as first-year credit) and a sense of overpaying by making differentiated service visible |
| Price buyer | Differentiation unneeded, search easy | Respond with a version stripped of cost down to the bare specification. Make it unattractive to existing customers and explainable. Walk away if it does not pay |
| Convenience buyer | Differentiation unneeded, search hard | Objections are rare. Provide ease and charge for it |

A value buyer posing as a price buyer shows these signs: "vague specifications" and "no commitment to award on lowest price." The countermeasure is to state publicly that "we do not bid until the specification is final."

### Professional buyer tactics and remedies

| Tactic | Remedy |
|---|---|
| Commoditizing the offer (sealed bids, reverse auctions) | Respond with a version that meets but does not exceed the spec, and charge for rush delivery and the like. Skip sham bids or bid at list price |
| Demanding a discount on the increase rate (a large account with a 25% discount wants a 6% increase cut to 4.5%) | The base is already discounted, so the increase amount is already smaller ($4.50, not $6). Make no exception on the rate; concede on timing |
| Volume discount ("we'll buy 10% more, so take 2% off everything") | Show the effective discount rate on the increment (22%), and pay the discount only on the increment as a year-end rebate |
| Making up for past failures | Compensate with a lump sum or capped credit, and keep the price intact |

Against power buyers (buyers who use their purchase volume to demand the lowest price), use five tactics: make them compete (give them something only you can sell exclusively), quantify your value, offer dedicated supply stripped of unneeded cost, split the product line, and resist divide-and-conquer (lowest price only on condition of adopting all products).

## Procedure

### A. Write the discount and negotiation policy (before the first deal)

1. **Measure the current state.** Identify "outlaw" customers who buy markedly cheaper for the same product, volume, and terms. Use price band analysis, which looks at the spread of transaction prices under the same terms ([pricing-capability.md](pricing-capability.md)).
2. **Calculate pocket price.**
   ```
   pocket price = list price − on-invoice discounts − off-invoice concessions
   off-invoice concessions = volume rebates + payment-terms discounts + co-op funds + free shipping + free services + promotion costs …
   ```
   Check whether the spread across customers is explained by differences in value or cost to serve.
3. **Build a give–get table.** List pre-approved trade-offs and caps that sales can offer without approval (for example: multi-year contract → year-end rebate; incremental volume → discount on the increment; relaxed delivery → lower configuration; prepayment → payment-terms discount; published case study → added onboarding support).
4. **Build a no-discount list.** Discounts that show list price is fiction (such as on the list price of the core tier) and discounts that leak across segments.
5. **Set rules for recording exceptions.** Record each with a reason; fix the structure or policy for exceptions that recur.
6. **Check the effective discount rate of volume discounts.**
   ```
   effective discount rate on the increment = discount rate × (existing volume + incremental volume) / incremental volume
   e.g. 2% × 110 / 10 = 22%
   ```

### B. Respond to price objections

1. Ask what they are comparing against, and correct the reference value (the basis of comparison) if it is wrong ([value-communication.md](value-communication.md)).
2. Determine the buyer type.
3. Restate the basis of value using the buyer's own numbers ([economic-value.md](economic-value.md)).
4. If you lower price, do it through the give–get table or a lower configuration with reduced value. Never make the same offer cheaper.
5. If they keep pushing only on price, decide whether the deal is worth taking even at the price floor ([price-level.md](price-level.md)), and do not let that deal become a reference price for other customers.

### C. Manage price increases

1. Publish the industry-wide cost situation and causal link, the size of the increase, and the effective date. Give notice several months in advance.
2. "We won't roll it back for anyone unless we roll it back for every customer in the industry." Give key customers a transition guarantee: if their competitor gets better terms, they get the same terms retroactively.
3. Concede only on timing. Exempting only the customers who resisted teaches that "resistance pays."
4. When moving from flexible pricing (where sales has discount discretion), start with the outlaws. A senior executive tells them "this is unfair to other customers," and sales saves the account with a give–get. If they accept a long-term contract, split the increase into three steps. Walk away from price buyers who will not.

### D. Promotions and recessions

1. Limit promotions to inducing trial, and use channels that reach only prospects. Pulsed promotions that regular customers learn to expect lower future willingness to pay and only borrow demand from the future.
2. Prefer money-back and performance guarantees over discounts to hold price.
3. First-year discounts on services feel like a loss at renewal and invite churn. Keep the price and induce with free gifts or credits.
4. In a recession, do not cut price to take share from close competitors. Index price to a customer-market indicator in exchange for long-term contracts, and take volume from strictly fenced new segments.

## Checklist

- [ ] The basis for discounts is explained by conditions set in advance, not by an approval hierarchy alone
- [ ] There is a give–get table (with caps) and a no-discount list
- [ ] There are rules for recording exceptions and for reflecting recurring exceptions in the price structure
- [ ] Pocket price per customer is calculated including off-invoice concessions
- [ ] Volume discounts apply only to the increment, and the effective discount rate was checked
- [ ] Objection handling is split by buyer type
- [ ] The price increase plan has a rationale, notice timing, a rollback rule, and timing-based concessions
- [ ] Legal issues such as exchanging price information were checked in [ethics-legal.md](ethics-legal.md)

## Common mistakes and fixes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Calling an approval hierarchy a pricing policy | Replace it with a condition-based give–get table |
| Discounting unconditionally for fear of losing the deal | Present a give–get to find out what the objection really is. If needed, proactively cut price for all eligible customers |
| Letting large accounts discount the increase rate | Show it is double-dipping on the discount, and concede on timing |
| Judging profitability on invoice price alone | Derive pocket price with the waterfall, and tighten loosely monitored concessions such as free shipping first |
| Declaring "no more discounts" with no transition plan | Correct gradually, starting with the outlaws |
