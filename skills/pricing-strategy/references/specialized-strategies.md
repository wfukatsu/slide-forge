# Strategies for life cycle, exchange rates, recessions, and transfer pricing

How to respond to four situations where normal pricing practice does not work: the category life cycle, export prices in foreign currency, market downturns, and transfer pricing. It is material for reworking the decisions at each step of the value cascade (value creation → value communication → price structure → price policy → price level → price competition) to fit the situation. Use it when pricing a new product, when the stage changes, when exchange rates swing, when demand falls, and when reviewing transfer pricing.

## Key points
- All four situations require reworking normal habits, so senior management or a pricing leader with authority decides across functions ([1] p.240).
- An innovation (a product that delivers benefits customers could not get before) does not sell on low price. Price as if early adopters (the segment that tries first and that later customers ask for advice) are the whole market, and set a high reference price (the price customers use to judge whether something is expensive or cheap). Spend the resulting margin on removing non-price barriers to adoption.
- In growth, prices fall and segmentation becomes the source of profit. In maturity, do not buy share. Protect margin with five moves: unbundling, better estimates of price sensitivity, revisiting cost allocation, complementary products, and channel re-evaluation.
- The response to exchange rates depends on two axes: market strategy (opportunistic or committed) and scope of impact (whether competitors are affected too, or only you). For a committed strategy facing a strong home currency, the only answer is to change strategy (for example, local production), not price.
- Across-the-board price cuts in a recession are the worst move. Separate falling demand into "fewer customers" and "lower differentiation value (the difference in value over the next-best competitive alternative)", and respond with mechanisms that bring prices back automatically when the market recovers.
- For a firm that buys components at a unit price, the supplier's fixed costs and profit become its own variable costs (non-incremental costs made incremental). This makes it weaker on price cuts than a vertically integrated competitor. Fix this in contract design.
- Bundling, network effects, and dynamic pricing are not covered. For bundles and price metrics see [price-structure.md](price-structure.md); for yield management see [pricing-policy.md](pricing-policy.md).

## Criteria and choices

### Life-cycle stage plan
| Stage | Trigger | Price moves | Structure moves | Metrics to monitor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | 12–24 months from launch, or until the first N reference customers | Establish a value-based reference price. Fence introductory incentives by period, volume, and in-kind service | Keep tiers simple and bundle generous support services | Barriers to adoption, reference customers won |
| Growth | Competitor entry, buyers becoming experienced | Do not cut across the board. Hold list price and lower prices through segment-specific offers | Add tiers and price fences (conditions for qualifying for a lower price). Unbundle high-cost services | Win/loss against new entrants, ratio of first-time to experienced buyers |
| Maturity | Category growth slows, imitation and standardization | Defend margin. Refine price-sensitivity estimates with repeat-purchase data | Unbundling, complementary products and optional services, channel review | Cost to serve, price band, revenue from complementary products |
| Decline | Permanent contraction (technology substitution) | With an advantage, take margin from loyal segments. Without one, harvest short-term cash | Simplify, prune products and customers | Exit economics, willingness to pay of remaining segments |

### Non-price barriers to adoption and responses (introduction)
| Barrier | Example responses |
|---|---|
| Distributors reluctant to carry it | Temporarily increase trade discounts to secure distributor margin (check the legal treatment of resale prices in [ethics-legal.md](ethics-legal.md)) |
| Doubt that the benefits are real | Strong purchase support, time-limited free trials, case studies, calculation tools |
| Risk of failure | Money-back guarantees, phased rollout, outcome guarantees where measurable |
| Effort of switching | Migration support, data migration services |

### Conditions that make price wars likely in growth
Large economies of scale, volume sets the industry standard, capacity exceeds demand ([1] p.244). If any of these apply, work out the response in [price-competition.md](price-competition.md).

### 2×2 for exchange-rate adjustment
| | Competitors are affected too (the buyer's currency moved) | Only you are affected (your home currency moved) |
|---|---|---|
| Opportunistic (picks up incremental sales; prices off the home currency) | Adjust local-currency prices quickly and fully to the exchange rate | Change prices and sales targets to optimize global contribution margin (price − unit incremental cost). Size price cuts using the breakeven sales change (the percentage change in volume needed to keep total contribution after the price change) |
| Committed (builds brand equity; prices off local competitor prices) | Keep relative prices and adjust to competitors' moves. Act so that low-share competitors are forced to raise prices or exit | Hold prices for now and buy time with hedges (options or futures). Then change strategy (a natural hedge by producing in weak-currency countries). Manage the gray market (redistribution outside authorized channels) |

The right column assumes a strong home currency. With a weak home currency, it makes sense to lower local prices and take share ([1] p.250).

### Remedies for transfer pricing
| Method | When to use |
|---|---|
| Pay fixed costs as a lump sum (design, tooling, and so on) | Components with large development or equipment fixed costs |
| Volume-tiered pricing (after fixed costs are recovered, extra volume is priced at incremental cost plus profit) | Volume is expected to grow |
| Profit sharing (a small fee plus a fixed share of the contribution margin on final sales) | Suppliers facing little competition that can demand high profits. The two methods above do not stop the supplier's profit from becoming incremental |

Example: for an independent firm buying components with a 30% contribution margin ratio, the breakeven for a 10% price cut is +50% sales; for an integrated firm making them in-house (70%), it is +16.7% ([1] Exhibit 10-4, 10-5).

## Steps
1. Identify the category's stage. Distinguish an innovation from an improvement on existing benefits, and match against the triggers in the stage table.
2. For an innovation, estimate total economic value (price of the next-best competitive alternative + differentiation value) for early adopters using the method in [economic-value.md](economic-value.md). Set the reference price within a range where buyers will not feel afterward that it "wasn't worth it".
3. List the barriers to adoption and choose responses from the non-price barriers table. Fund them from the high margin at introduction.
4. In growth and maturity, turn the structure moves in the stage table into a price list. If considering a price cut, test it with the breakeven sales change (formulas in [financial-analysis.md](financial-analysis.md)).
5. When exchange rates move, first check whether the cause is the buyer's currency or your home currency. Then confirm your market strategy and determine the cell in the 2×2.
6. When demand falls, decide whether it is a permanent contraction or a cyclical downturn. If cyclical, separate "fewer customers" from "lower value" for each segment. Limit price cuts to price-sensitive segments, and use value-linked price metrics (the unit in which the price is charged) or retroactive rebates. Also consider raising the value delivered instead of cutting price (for example, guarantees that remove risk).
7. If purchased components are a large cost, estimate the fixed costs and profit included in the supplier's unit price. Compare contribution margins with integrated competitors and, if needed, negotiate transfer-pricing remedies.

## Checklist
- [ ] The category's stage and the evidence (trigger) for it are stated
- [ ] The price of an innovation is based on value to early adopters and does not create a low reference price through penetration pricing (a strategy of taking volume and share with low prices)
- [ ] Introductory incentives are fenced by period, volume, or in-kind, and list price is maintained
- [ ] Non-price barriers to adoption are listed, each with a response and a budget
- [ ] There is a record of considering the five maturity moves (unbundling, price sensitivity, cost allocation, complementary products, channels)
- [ ] For exchange rates, both the cause of the change (which currency) and the market strategy are identified
- [ ] The recession response is segment-specific, not an across-the-board cut, and is designed so prices return on recovery
- [ ] The transfer-pricing evaluation checks that supplier fixed costs and profit are not treated as variable costs

## Common mistakes and fixes
- **Launching at a low price to speed adoption.** Imitators have no basis for comparison, so they do not see it as a bargain. Only a low reference price remains. Hold the price and invest in removing risk and providing information.
- **Buying share with price in maturity.** Competitors match and only margin disappears. Unbundle, focusing on where you have an advantage.
- **Waiting for competitors to raise prices when the home currency is strong.** Competitors in other countries will not raise prices. Hedges only buy time; review production locations and market strategy.
- **Cutting list price in a recession.** Buyers keep expecting that price after recovery. Hold list price and use conditional rebates tied to measures such as industry capacity utilization.
- **Treating accounting fixed costs as fixed.** Many are semi-fixed (costs that need additions when volume grows substantially). In plans with large volume changes, treat them as incremental costs.
