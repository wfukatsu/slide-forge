# Value and price communication
This document covers how to communicate a product's value so customers understand it and accept the price. It is the second tier of the value cascade (value creation → value communication → price structure → price policy → price level → price competition), the tier that turns economic value into perceived value. Read it when economic value is ample but customers still say "it's expensive," or when designing value messages, value calculators, price presentation, or B2B messages per stakeholder.

## Key points
- Value communication has three goals: make customers understand the benefits, raise willingness to pay (the most a customer is willing to pay), and raise the probability of purchase.
- Value does not get across on its own. Sellers tend to fall back on feature lists, and B2B purchasing staff find it easier to justify the cheaper option internally. The seller's job is to give buyers material to justify the premium internally ([1] pp.56–57).
- Choose the message on two axes: the type of benefit (economic or psychological) and the buyer's involvement (how deeply they deliberate over the purchase). Use quantitative messages based on EVE (economic value estimation; [economic-value.md](economic-value.md)) for economic benefits, and emotional messages for psychological benefits.
- Communication pays off most when the gap between perceived value (the value the buyer actually recognizes) and economic value is large: customers with little market experience, innovative benefits, and benefits that are easy to miss (such as inventory reduction from short lead times).
- Real buyers decide by heuristics (rules of thumb). Nine effects move price sensitivity systematically: reference competitor, switching cost, difficult comparison, end benefit, price-quality, expenditure, shared cost, transaction value, and fairness ([1] pp.64–73).
- The same price can look cheap or expensive depending on the reference point (decoys, a different comparison, restating the unit). A decoy is a high-priced option placed to make other options look reasonable.
- In B2B, break company-wide value down into gains and losses per stakeholder, and deliver it to the right person at the right time.

## Decision criteria

### Involvement × benefit type
| | Economic benefit | Psychological benefit |
|---|---|---|
| High involvement | Quantitative value argument: customer-specific EVE, value calculators, TCO (total cost of ownership) comparisons, references. E.g. management consulting, aircraft engines, medical devices | Reassurance and proof: demos, guarantees, peer recommendations, trial memberships, showing the risk of failure. E.g. weight-loss programs, gifts |
| Low involvement | Short economic claims at the point of purchase ("save X yen a year," "twice the life"). E.g. energy-saving bulbs, long-life batteries | Framing and cues: brand, ease of trial, packaging, reference points. E.g. cosmetics, household goods |

Involvement rises with the amount spent and with use in situations others can see. The same product can fall in different quadrants depending on the purchase occasion. Most purchases have both elements, so for products with both economic and psychological benefits, combine both kinds of message. Most new B2B products fall in the upper left (high involvement, economic benefit), so build a value calculator from the EVE algorithms so sales can enter the customer's numbers on the spot.

### Nine effects on price sensitivity
| Effect | Sensitivity falls when | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Reference competitor | The comparison is to high-priced items, or the product looks mid-range | State the alternative being replaced and its total cost. Place a decoy above |
| Switching cost | Switching requires product-specific reinvestment | Quantify and show the burden of certification, training, and migration |
| Difficult comparison | Alternatives are hard to compare | Stress track record and reputation. Do not invite unit-price comparisons yourself |
| End benefit | The end result is large and failure is costly | Portray the product as a small cost within a large outcome |
| Price-quality | There are no other quality cues, or exclusivity is valued | Do not destroy the quality signal with cheap sales |
| Expenditure | The amount or its share of the budget is small | Restate it per day or per case |
| Shared cost | The buyer pays only a small share (insurance, expenses, tax deductions) | Show how to get reimbursed or expense it |
| Transaction value | The payment feels lower than the reference price (the price the buyer considers reasonable and fair) | Show list price first, and present the discount visibly |
| Fairness | The reason for a price increase looks legitimate, such as higher costs | Avoid increases based on market power. For the same amount, remove a discount rather than add a surcharge |

## Procedure
1. Measure the gap between perceived value and economic value. Put the total economic value from [economic-value.md](economic-value.md) next to perceived value inferred from customer comments and loss reasons. If the gap is large, communication is the main lever.
2. Determine the quadrant per segment. Set involvement and benefit type, and pick the message format from the table above.
3. Set the reference point. Show first what is being replaced and its total cost, and have customers compare the price with that, not with a feature table.
4. Restate the unit of comparison in the unit where customers feel value, and show that differentiation value exceeds the price difference.
   ```
   Unit price = annual price / (users × working days)   e.g. per user per day
   Net effect = differentiation value − price difference
     e.g. Drug-eluting stent (price $3,500, twice the conventional type)
          restenosis rate difference 15% × repeat surgery $30,000 = $4,500 > price difference $1,750
   ```
5. For economic benefits, build a value calculator ([1] Exhibit 3-3). Separate inputs (assumed to be replaced with the customer's own numbers) from calculations (amount per driver and the total), and make every assumption visible. Make it something you can revise together with the customer.
6. For psychological benefits, keep estimated amounts out of the message. Make an impression with high-value benefits the customer had not considered, or with performance that is hard to judge before experiencing it (guarantees, demos, recommendations).
7. Decide how to present the price. Place a deliberate premium option above the recommended plan. Explain the reasons for a high price (scarce materials, service level, guarantees). Make discounts visible after showing list price.
8. In B2B, break it down by stakeholder. Mark + and − in a stakeholder × value element matrix ([1] Exhibit 3-4), and decide on individual messages for opponents and the indifferent, and at which point in the buying process to deliver each.
9. Refer handling discount requests and give–get (rules for getting something back from the customer in exchange for concessions such as discounts) to [pricing-policy.md](pricing-policy.md).

## Checklist
- [ ] Messages are written as benefits to the customer's economics or feelings, not as features
- [ ] Each segment's involvement × benefit-type quadrant is stated
- [ ] The alternative being replaced and its total cost are shown as the reference point
- [ ] Economic benefit claims have formulas, assumptions, and sources
- [ ] Value calculator inputs can be replaced with the customer's numbers
- [ ] Psychological benefits are not forced into monetary figures
- [ ] The price unit matches how customers feel value
- [ ] Reasons for a high price are explained, and increases do not take a form that undermines fairness
- [ ] Gains and losses per B2B stakeholder, and a message for each, are covered
- [ ] The material lets purchasing staff justify the premium internally

## Common mistakes and fixes
| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Listing specs and leaving the translation into benefits to the buyer | Translate in the order feature → benefit → amount (or feeling) |
| Cutting price because perceived value is low | Educate while holding price. Cut price only when economic value itself falls short |
| Putting weakly supported numbers on psychological value | It varies widely by individual, so do not quantify it; communicate with proof and experience |
| Selling only to purchasing staff | Reach the line managers who experience the value, and give purchasing staff material for justification |
| Pushing for a decision on the first offer, which lowers transaction value | Show list price and value first, and present terms as concessions with something in return |
| Running repeated discount campaigns | They destroy the price-quality effect and the reference price. Respond with structure (a lower tier) ([price-structure.md](price-structure.md)) |
| Raising prices on the strength of market power when demand is high | Consider the effect on future business. Choose the reason and the form of the increase (removing discounts, adjusting capacity) |
