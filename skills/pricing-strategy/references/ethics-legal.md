# Pricing ethics and legal constraints

The ethical and legal issues in pricing actions. These constraints apply to every step of the value cascade (value creation → value communication → price structure → price policy → price level → price competition). Use this in the review before finalizing a pricing strategy, discount policy, resale or distribution program, or competitive response. It is a list of issues to take to legal review, not legal advice. The legal content assumes US antitrust law; always have local counsel check matters in Japan or the EU.

## Key points
- Examine a price proposal from three angles: "Is it profitable?", "Will it be seen as ethical?", and "Is it legal?" Many problematic policies can be made both profitable and defensible with small changes.
- Draw the ethical line at one of five levels. Decide where before facing an ambiguous situation, and write it into the discount policy.
- Price agreements among competitors (horizontal price fixing) are per se illegal (illegal once the conduct is shown). Also avoid exchanging price information and conduct that suggests an agreement.
- Resale price agreements have been judged under the rule of reason (weighing pro-competitive against anti-competitive effects) since *Khan* (1997) and *Leegin* (2007). A unilateral policy without agreement is safer under the *Colgate* doctrine.
- Price discrimination (Robinson-Patman Act) is illegal only when all five elements are present. It covers only goods, and customers who do not compete with each other are excluded. There are three defenses (cost justification, meeting competition, changing conditions).
- Promotional discrimination is stricter than price discrimination. Promotions must be offered to competing resellers on proportionally equal terms.
- Predatory pricing rarely succeeds as a claim, because it requires proving pricing below cost plus a dangerous probability of recouping the losses. Internal documents describing penetration pricing (a strategy of taking volume and share with low prices) as "to eliminate competitors" are dangerous, however.
- Keeping records of the basis for prices (cost differences, competitor prices, value differences) is the precondition for every defense.

## Criteria and choices

### Five levels of ethics (conditions are cumulative)
| Level | Condition for a transaction to be ethical | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The price is paid voluntarily | No coercion. "Let the buyer beware" |
| 2 | Plus, information is symmetric | Do not sell while hiding known defects or risks |
| 3 | Plus, no exploiting necessity | Do not take excessive profit on necessities or in emergencies |
| 4 | Plus, the price is justified by cost | Value-based segmented pricing becomes questionable |
| 5 | Plus, anyone can obtain it regardless of ability to pay | Almost incompatible with market prices |

Value-based segmented pricing (the central method throughout [1]) is ethically sustainable only up to level 3. Check which level your company, industry, and customers each stand at.

### US legal issues
| Issue | What to check | Guideline |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal price fixing (Sherman Act Section 1) | Any agreement or information exchange with competitors on prices, terms, or discounts | Per se illegal. Publishing your own prices is fine. Parallel conduct plus "plus factors" (such as acts that would be against self-interest alone but profitable if all do them) supports an inference of agreement |
| Price signaling | Announcing pricing intentions in advance | Usually legal if there is a legitimate purpose such as helping buyers plan (*DuPont*). Avoid collusive or exclusionary uses |
| Resale price maintenance | Telling resellers what price to charge | Agreements are judged under the rule of reason. Announcing a unilateral policy and stopping sales to violators (*Colgate*) is legal. Warnings, threats, and "probation" suggest an agreement |
| Minimum advertised price (MAP) | Tying advertising allowances to advertised prices | Judged under the rule of reason. Offer on equal terms to competing resellers |
| Price discrimination (Robinson-Patman Act) | Selling comparable goods to competing buyers at different prices | Illegal when all five elements below are present. Defenses: cost justification (up to actual savings), meeting competition (good-faith belief), changing conditions (perishable, seasonal, obsolete goods, and so on) |
| Promotional discrimination | Whether promotional funds or services are functionally available to all competing resellers | Proportionally equal terms are required. The only defense is meeting competition |
| Vertical non-price restraints | Restricting customers, territories, or products | Judged under the rule of reason (*Sylvania*). Allowed when they promote interbrand competition |
| Exclusive dealing | Prohibiting purchase of other firms' products | Legal unless it unreasonably forecloses competitors |
| Tying | Making purchase of one product a condition for buying another | Bundles whose components are sold separately usually do not qualify. Coercion by a firm with market power is dangerous |
| Predatory pricing (Sherman Act Section 2) | Pricing below incremental cost (marginal cost) aimed at eliminating competitors | Requires both below-cost pricing and a dangerous probability of recoupment (*Brooke Group*) |
| Accounting controls (Sarbanes-Oxley Act) | Whether discounts and promotional funds are covered by internal controls | Define documentation and investigation procedures |
| Deceptive reference pricing (outside the scope of [1]) | Whether "regular price" and "compare at" prices are based on real, recent prices | Check FTC guides and state law. Do not use fictitious reference prices |

### Five elements of price discrimination ([1] p.303)
1. Discrimination: prices differ by customer. It does not apply if some customers simply do not use a discount available to all.
2. Sales to two or more buyers: actual sales close in time. Quotes alone do not count.
3. Commodities: goods only, not services.
4. Like grade and quality: physically and substantially the same product.
5. Reasonable probability of competitive injury: injury to competitors (primary line) or to disfavored resellers (secondary line). In the secondary line, only customers who compete with each other must be treated equally.

## Steps
1. Identify the jurisdictions where you sell and state them in the report.
2. Decide the ethical level. Where necessities, emergencies, or vulnerable customers are involved, check the price proposal against that level.
3. Apply the US legal issues table from the top and rate each relevant issue high, medium, or low risk.
4. For each price difference between segments or channels, record the justification: a cost difference, meeting a competitor's price, a product or service difference, or non-competing customers.
5. Document the resale price policy as a unilateral policy, not an agreement. Limit the response to violations to stopping sales of the product concerned.
6. Check that promotional programs have alternatives available to all competing resellers.
7. Apply the Japan and EU checks (below). List those that apply for attorney review.

## Checklist
- [ ] The jurisdictions are stated, with a note that this is "not legal advice"
- [ ] The chosen ethical level is written into the discount policy
- [ ] There is no contact with competitors about prices or terms (including through trade associations)
- [ ] There is no agreement, commitment, or probation regarding resale prices
- [ ] A cost, competition, or value justification is kept for each price difference
- [ ] Promotional funds are offered to competing resellers on proportionally equal terms
- [ ] Bundle components are also available separately
- [ ] Introductory prices are above incremental cost, or the reason and period for going below are recorded
- [ ] Comparison or reference price claims are backed by actual sales history
- [ ] When selling in Japan or the EU, a request for attorney review is in the plan

## Japan and EU checks (outside the scope of [1]; attorney review required)
[1] covers only US law. The following are indicative issues to check; their accuracy is not guaranteed.
- **Japan's Antimonopoly Act (resale price restriction)**: stricter than the US and treated as illegal in principle. Keep suggested retail prices as "reference" only, and do not impose disadvantages to enforce them.
- **Japan's Antimonopoly Act (unjust low-price sales)**: check that you are not continuously selling at prices well below the cost of supply.
- **Japan's Antimonopoly Act (discriminatory pricing)**: check that you are not setting unjustly different prices by region or counterparty.
- **Japan's Antimonopoly Act (abuse of a superior bargaining position)**: where your trading position is strong, check that you are not imposing price reductions, requests for sponsorship payments, forced returns, and the like.
- **Japan's Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations (double pricing)**: check that a comparison price has a track record as "a price at which the product was sold over a reasonably recent period".
- **EU competition law**: resale price maintenance is treated as a hardcore restriction. Discriminatory pricing, predatory pricing, and tying by dominant firms are also strictly regulated. Watch for restrictions on parallel trade between territories.

## Common mistakes and fixes
- **Legal becomes overly conservative and bans all value-based price differences.** Go through the elements one by one with the issues table and show that non-competing customers and services are outside the scope.
- **Warning a reseller "we will stop dealing with you next time."** A warning suggests an agreement. Publish the policy and, on violation, quietly stop selling.
- **Giving promotional funds only to large accounts.** Provide alternatives and proportional terms to competing smaller resellers too.
- **Building a resale policy for Japan on US assumptions.** Japan treats resale price restriction very differently. Check with local counsel.
