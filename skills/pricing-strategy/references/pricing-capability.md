# Building strategic pricing capability

How to set up the people, organizational structure, and data and analytics tools an organization needs to execute a pricing strategy. It is the foundation that makes the strategies decided at each step of the value cascade (value creation → value communication → price structure → price policy → price level → price competition) execute consistently in the field. Use it when strategy is not executed in the field, when discounts are out of control, when redesigning the pricing organization or sales compensation, or when diagnosing capability.

## Key points
- Capability depends on the balance of three pillars: people, organizational structure (decision rights, processes, incentives), and data and analytics tools. Investing in only one does not produce results.
- Firms that combine a value-based strategy with strong execution (Value Masters) earn operating profit 1.24 times the industry average. Firms with strong execution alone (Runaway Trains) come last at 0.91 times ([1] p.263). Strong execution in the wrong direction only makes losses faster and larger.
- The biggest reason good strategies fail is that processes and incentives conflict with strategic goals.
- Set the degree of centralization by business diversity. The three options are centralized, center of expertise, and dedicated support unit.
- Make decision rights explicit in four types: Input (provide information), Make (decide), Ratify (approve or overturn), and Notify (be informed). Give Make and Ratify to different people.
- Sales compensation proportional to revenue encourages discounts that erode profit. Switching to sales credit adjusted by a profitability factor (kicker) ties pay to profit without disclosing costs.
- Find profit leaks with two kinds of analysis: customer analysis (win-loss analysis, customer profitability) and process analysis (price bands, price waterfall).
- Strategy sets system requirements; never let the system set the strategy. Driving change requires executives whose actions match their words and demonstration projects with a control group.

## Criteria and choices

### Organizational archetypes
| Archetype | Where decisions sit | Role of headquarters | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Centralized Pricing | Headquarters | Decides and controls. Business units collect data and comply | Single market. Commodity-like industries. Industries where one price change spills into other markets (such as airlines) |
| Center of Expertise | Business units | Advanced analytics, sharing best practices, internal consulting | Markets with strong local conditions (such as retail and telecom) |
| Dedicated Support Unit | A dedicated group in each business unit | Loose coordination only | Diversified firms with little market overlap |

### Example decision-rights assignment
| Right | Typical holder | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Input | Finance, demand forecasting, field sales, market research | Not accountable for commercial results |
| Make | Pricing leader, or one committee | Limit holders to one person or one committee |
| Ratify | Senior management | Overturns only decisions that conflict with company-wide priorities |
| Notify | Product development, operations, billing | Notify people who use the decision in their own work |

### Choosing analytics tools
| Analysis | Question it answers | Next move |
|---|---|---|
| Win-loss analysis | Do we win or lose against competitors, and why? | Fix either price or differentiation. The goal is profit contribution, not win rate |
| Customer profitability map | Who is profitable and who is not (by price and cost to serve per customer) | Protect small high-margin customers by understanding why they pay a premium. Target large low-profit customers for price increases or cost reduction |
| Price band | In deals with the same conditions, who deviates from the peer price (the price found by regressing on legitimate factors such as volume, service, and region)? | Form and test hypotheses on the causes of outliers and correct them through policy |
| Price waterfall | Subtracting discounts, rebates, payment terms, free services, and so on in turn from list price, what is the pocket price (the price actually left after all deductions)? | Tighten operating rules at the leakiest steps (such as requiring paperwork to waive shipping) |

### System functions
Commercial systems have four functions: price analytics, price optimization (effective in markets where all competitor prices are visible), price management (bulk price-list updates through ERP integration), and deal management (quotes and approval records within policy). Standardize analysis in spreadsheets or BI tools before considering dedicated software.

## Steps
1. Ask the top five executives to explain the company's pricing strategy individually. If the answers differ, the strategy is not shared.
2. Assess each of the six elements of the value cascade (value creation, value communication, price structure, price policy, price level, price competition) on three dimensions: clarity of strategy, organizational alignment and ownership, and whether systems exist. Rate maturity on five levels: Basic, Reactive, Progressive, Optimized, Preeminent ([1] Exhibit 11-4). The content of each element is in [value-cascade.md](value-cascade.md).
3. Choose the organizational archetype from business diversity. Then assign Input, Make, Ratify, and Notify in a table for each major pricing decision.
4. Set up the pricing process in four stages. First define the key activities (include opportunity assessment, price setting, negotiation, charging for rush orders, contracting, and billing). Next map the current flow. Then find where profit leaks. Finally redesign it, including decision rights.
5. For everyone with decision rights, document current performance metrics to expose conflicts. Introduce sales credit for sales staff. The formula is "sales credit = [target price − k × (target price − actual price)] × volume", where k is at least the reciprocal of the contribution margin ratio (contribution margin ÷ price, where contribution margin is price − unit incremental cost) at the target price. Example: at a 20% contribution margin ratio, k = 5, and a 15% discount cuts the credit by 75% ([1] p.278).
6. Collect 12–36 months of transaction data covering list price, invoice price, discounts, rebates, returns, shipping, service consumption, and per-customer P&L. Then run price waterfall, price band, and customer profitability analyses.
7. Design a demonstration project with a narrow scope and period and a single metric. Prepare two similar customer groups and apply the new policy to only one. Show success early, then roll it out company-wide.
8. Define the executives' role: mandatory training, regular reviews, visible support, and not making exceptions themselves.

## Checklist (capability diagnosis)
- [ ] Top executives explain the pricing strategy in the same words
- [ ] Each of the six value cascade elements has a maturity rating and an owner
- [ ] The organizational archetype (centralized, center of expertise, dedicated support unit) fits the business structure
- [ ] Input, Make, Ratify, and Notify are documented for each major pricing decision, and Make and Ratify are separate
- [ ] The pricing process covers not only discount approval but also negotiation, rush charges, contracting, and billing
- [ ] Discounts have predefined conditions (give–get: what the customer gives in exchange for the discount). Ad hoc approval is not the default (see [pricing-policy.md](pricing-policy.md))
- [ ] Sales compensation is tied to a profitability-adjusted metric, not revenue
- [ ] Conflicts between departmental metrics (revenue, share, gross margin, utilization) are understood
- [ ] Transaction-level price data (down to pocket price) can be analyzed for 12 months or more
- [ ] Win-loss, customer profitability, price bands, and price waterfall are reviewed regularly
- [ ] System investment requirements are derived from the pricing strategy
- [ ] New initiatives are tested in experiments with a control group before rollout

## Common mistakes and fixes
- **Investing in tools first.** Airlines lost their cumulative profits to date in price wars fought with yield management tools ([1] p.265). Set strategy and organization first, then define tool requirements.
- **Executives allowing "just this once" exceptions.** Exceptions become routine and the strategy is abandoned. Allow exceptions only through the Ratify procedure.
- **Finance rejecting deals against a uniform gross margin threshold.** Sales starts spending time on workarounds. Switch to criteria that account for value and segment, and constrain with policies such as payment terms.
- **Targeting win rate.** Lowering prices raises win rate. Check whether the volume gain made up for the margin loss.
- **Looking only at invoice price.** Most leakage happens after the invoice (payment terms, free services, rebates). Follow the price waterfall down to pocket price.
- **Making the demonstration too broad.** Interest fades and cause and effect become unclear. Narrow the period, scope, and metric, and use a control group.
